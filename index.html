Block 2

Scale Degrees & Personalities

The In-Depth Analysis · Art of Slow Blues · BluesBlock Method

You've already done the hard part.

In the Scale Degree Explorer challenge, you sat with each note and listened, not for right answers, but for the feeling each one produced. Some settled. Some leaned. Some made you wince a little. You weren't analysing; you were noticing. And everything you noticed was true.

This lesson doesn't teach you something new. It gives you the language for what you already heard.

From Addresses to Personalities

Here is a thought experiment.

I tell you I live at 42 Church Street. That's an address. It tells you exactly where I am, you can find it on a map, you can navigate to it. But it tells you nothing about what it feels like to walk through the door. Whether the street is quiet or busy. Whether the neighbours are warm. Whether you'd feel at home there or uneasy.

Now imagine I tell you instead: I live in a small terraced house on a narrow street near an old stone church, the kind of street where people still nod to each other in the mornings.

Same location. Completely different understanding.

That is the difference between a note name and a scale degree.

When you learn that there's a note on the G string at the fifth fret, you've learned an address. You can find it. That's genuinely useful, you need to be able to find a note before you can play it. But the address tells you nothing about what that note means in context. Whether it wants to move or can rest. Whether it aches or opens. Whether it belongs where you've placed it or wants somewhere else entirely.

A scale degree gives you all of that. Not just where the note is, but who it is.

And here is the practical magic that flows from this: scale degrees don't change when you change key. The ♭3 always feels like the ♭3. It always aches in the same direction. Learn it in A minor and you know it in every key, not as a shape to transpose, but as a feeling you can rely on.

Notes are addresses. Scale degrees are personalities. And personalities don't change when you move house.

The Cast of Characters

Think of the fretboard not as a grid of positions to memorise, but as a cast of characters, each one with a distinct personality, an emotional quality, a way of behaving that you can learn to recognise and trust.

You already met them in the ear training exercise. Here are their names.

1 The Root Home

The stable point. Every phrase either departs from the root or returns to it, sometimes both. When you land on the root at the end of a phrase, the listener exhales. They may not know why, but they feel it. The root doesn't do dramatic things. It doesn't need to. It's home.

♭3 The 𝘣3 The Ache

The dark heart of the minor blues scale. This is the note that makes the blues sound like the blues, that characteristic colour that sits somewhere between melancholy and longing. Over the i chord it pulls gently toward the root, unresolved, leaning. Over the iv chord it takes on a different flavour, more restless, more searching.

B.B. King made a career out of bending toward the ♭3 and holding it just long enough for the listener to feel it in their chest. That feeling has a name now.

4 The 4 Questioning

A note that creates movement and inquiry. It doesn't sit still, it leans forward into the next phrase, asking something. Used at the start of a line, it creates anticipation. Used at the end, it leaves something open, unfinished. The 4 is not a note you linger on. It is a note that moves.

♭5 The ♭5 The Outsider

The blue note. Raw, slightly dangerous, unresolved. The ♭5 doesn't play well with others, it creates real tension when you hold it, and that tension is exactly its value. Use it deliberately. Pass through it with intention and it sounds like the blues. Sit on it too long without knowing why and it sounds like a mistake. The difference is always intention.

5 The 5 Stable

A breath of air in a phrase that has been building. The 5 is resolved, open, reliable. After tension, after the ♭3 or the ♭5 has done its work, the 5 gives the listener something to stand on. It is not as settled as the root, but it is comfortable enough to rest on briefly. Think of it as a ledge rather than a landing.

♭7 The ♭7 Yearning

One step short of home. The ♭7 is always reaching, it has enough resolution to feel like arrival, but not quite enough to feel like rest. It is, in many ways, the quintessential blues note. The feeling of being close to something without quite having it. Every guitarist who has ever bent toward the ♭7 and held it knows that feeling in their hands before they know it in theory.

The Same Note, Different Chord

You just met the cast. Six personalities, six feelings, each one earned by listening rather than being told.

Sit with that for a second, because it's about to get more complicated.

Every one of those personalities was measured against a single chord, the i chord. But a twelve bar blues doesn't stay on the i chord. The iv chord arrives. The V chord arrives. And when they do, does the ♭3 keep aching the way you just learned it aches? Does the ♭7 keep yearning?

Here is something worth sitting with.

Each of these personalities behaves slightly differently depending on the chord underneath it. You heard this in the V chord lesson, notes that felt comfortable over the i chord suddenly created real tension when the progression moved. That wasn't a mistake. That was the chord changing the meaning of the note.

Take the ♭3. Over the i chord it aches, gently, toward home. Over the iv chord it fully becomes the ♭7 of that chord, the same yearning, reaching quality you already know. Over the V chord it creates real tension, a pull that wants resolution the moment the chord moves on.

The note didn't change. The chord did.

Now you know your cast of characters in their positions over the i chord. Let's put them next to the other two chords and see what happens to each one.

Look at that table. Three chords, five degrees each, fifteen things to hold in your head if you memorise it the hard way. This is a place many might lay down their anchor, and that's not a discipline problem, it's a design problem.

Here's the lifeline. You already know five of these, the personalities you just met a moment ago, all of them measured against the i chord. You don't need fifteen facts. You need those five, plus one small piece of arithmetic.

Lay your five degrees on a clock with seven positions instead of twelve. The root sits at 1. The ♭3 sits at 3. Then 4, then 5, then the ♭7 sits at 7. Notice the numbers already agree with the names, the ♭3 lives at position 3, the ♭7 lives at position 7, the flat is only ever telling you about quality, never about where the hand lands. Two positions, 2 and ♭6, sit empty for now, you'll meet both of them again in a moment. When you count past 7, you don't fall off the clock, you just wrap back round to 1.

Here's the whole rule. Want the iv chord version of a degree, add 4. Want the V chord version, add 3. That's it.

And it runs the other way too. If you're already sitting up in the iv chord shape and you can feel the i chord about to arrive, you don't have to shift position to find a strong landing, subtract 4 instead, and whatever degree you're parked on tells you exactly what it becomes once the root returns. Subtract 3 does the same thing coming back from the V chord. This is the move the best players seem to make without thinking about it, staying exactly where the hand already is and reaching for resolution rather than jumping somewhere new every time the chord changes. Most players never think to reach for it on purpose. Now you have a rule for it.

Take the root, sitting at 1. Add 4 and it lands on 5. Add 3 and it lands on 4. Now take the ♭7, sitting at 7. Add 4 and it doesn't just move, it wraps straight past 7 and lands back round at 4, fully becoming Questioning, the same personality the 4 already carries over the i chord.

Now run it backwards. You're up in the iv chord shape, parked on what reads as a 5. The i chord is about to return. Subtract 4, and that same note turns out to be the root all along. Same finger, same fret. You didn't move. The meaning did.

Run that same piece of arithmetic across all five degrees and you get the whole table above, without ever having memorised fifteen separate facts. You worked them out.

One thing worth knowing before you take this to a backing track. The personality travels with the degree automatically, once you've learned it. Bump the root up to a 5 over the iv chord and it still carries that same settled, at home feeling, it's just relocated. You're not learning a new set of five personalities for every chord, you're learning five, once, and the rule hands you all fifteen for free.

Try it against a backing track before you move on. Play a degree over the i chord, name the number in your head, bump it forward the moment the chord changes. Then try it in reverse, park in a shape, and reach for the root as the i chord comes back around. Fifteen facts, or five facts and one small piece of arithmetic. You already know which one you'd rather carry into a solo.

Two Notes Outside the Five

Friendly Stranger. Bump the 5 forward for the iv chord and you land on the 9th, outside your five pentatonic notes. Don't go hunting for it in your BluesBlock shape. But don't avoid it either, blues chords get extended constantly in real playing, and a 9th showing up underneath you is a colour, not a mistake. It's just not what this rule is built to find on purpose.

Welcome Stranger. Bump the ♭3 forward for the V chord and you land on the ♭6, and this one isn't a rare guest. It's the same tension you'll meet properly when the V chord arrives later in this course, one of the most important places you can choose to land. The Chord Bump just told you where to find it, and what it's called.

The ♭5 doesn't appear on this clock at all. Friendly Stranger and Welcome Stranger are real degrees living outside your five. The ♭5 isn't a degree of the key, it's a chromatic passing tone, borrowed colour rather than a member of the scale, with no position to occupy because the clock was only ever built for the five that belong to it.

Practice the Chord Bump

Play as long as you like, no set number of questions. Forward moves you from the i chord into the iv or V chord. Reverse brings you back. Random throws everything into one session.
